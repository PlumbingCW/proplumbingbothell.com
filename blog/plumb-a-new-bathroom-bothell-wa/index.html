How to Plumb a New Bathroom: Rough-In Basics for Bothell

.

Plumbing a new bathroom is a sequence: drains first, then vents, then supply lines, then inspection. Getting the order and the slope right is what keeps it trouble free.

  • Drain and vent.
  • Supply lines.
  • Permit and inspection.

By the Bothell Pro Plumbing team.Published October 24, 2026.8 min read.
Short answer: Plumb a bathroom in order: set drain and vent lines first, run supply lines second, pressure test, get the rough-in inspected, then close the walls and set the fixtures. Drains need slope and every fixture needs a vent.

Start with the layout

.

Where each fixture goes decides everything else. The toilet needs the largest drain and should sit close to the main stack. The sink and tub can sit farther away but need their own traps and vents. Moving a toilet is the single most expensive layout change, so settle it first.

Check the floor framing too. A toilet drain cuts through joists, and a tub or shower needs a recess and support. A plan on paper saves time and cuts surprises.

The order of a bathroom rough-in

.
  1. 1

    Drain lines

    .

    Run the toilet, sink and tub or shower drains to the main stack or branch. Drains must slope toward the stack at a steady pitch, commonly a quarter inch per foot.

  2. 2

    Vent lines

    .

    Every fixture needs a vent so air can enter behind the draining water. Vents connect to the stack or run through the roof.

  3. 3

    Supply lines

    .

    Run hot and cold lines to each fixture, with shutoff valves where code requires them.

  4. 4

    Pressure test

    .

    Test the drains with water or air and the supply lines with pressure before anything is covered.

  5. 5

    Inspection

    .

    The rough-in is inspected before walls close. Fix any items the inspector notes.

  6. 6

    Set the fixtures

    .

    After walls and floors are finished, set the toilet, sink, tub and faucets and test each one.

Why slope and venting matter

.

A drain that slopes too little leaves solids behind, and one that slopes too much can let water run past the solids. A quarter inch per foot is the common target for small drains. Venting lets air in behind the water so it flows smoothly and the trap seal stays in place.

A badly vented fixture will gurgle, drain slowly and sometimes let sewer gas into the room. These problems are hard to fix after the walls close, which is why we plan the vent route at the start.

Basement bathrooms

.

If the bathroom sits below the main sewer line, gravity may not carry waste out. A sewage ejector pump collects waste in a basin and pumps it up to the drain. It adds cost and a maintenance item.

We check the depth of the main line before recommending a layout. A camera run helps, see sewer inspection.

Shower and tub details that cause trouble later

.

A shower pan or tub needs a watertight surface and a correct drain. The valve body must sit at the right depth for the trim, and access to it is wise. A leak behind tile is expensive to fix, so the pan is tested with water before tile goes on.

Ask that the valve location has an access panel on the back side when the layout allows. Years later, a failed cartridge is a short repair if you can reach it.

Choosing fixtures before the rough-in

.

Rough-in dimensions depend on the fixtures. A toilet needs a specific distance from the wall to the drain, and a vanity sink needs the supply and drain at the right height. Choose fixtures first or at least pick the models, so the plumber can set the pipes to match.

Ordering fixtures late is one of the most common reasons bathroom projects stall. Keep the spec sheets for each piece and give them to the plumber.

Before the walls close

.
  • Layout drawn and dimensions confirmed.
  • Fixture models chosen and spec sheets on hand.
  • Drain slope and vent routes checked.
  • Supply lines pressure tested.
  • Rough-in inspection passed under the permit.
  • Access to the shower valve planned.

Permits and inspections in Bothell

.

The City of Bothell Permit Center says plumbing work may need a permit. A new bathroom adds drain, vent and supply lines, so expect a permit and an inspection. Bothell Municipal Code exempts some work, such as repairing leaks or replacing a prefabricated fixture, but not new construction and not code violations.

Washington requires plumbing contractors to be licensed, and the state Department of Labor and Industries has a lookup tool. Permit and licensing rules for owners vary, so ask the Permit Center first. We handle the permit steps on jobs we do, see plumbing installation. New bathroom plumbing is permitted and inspected under the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted in WAC 51-56, which sets drain size, venting and slope.
